Minutes—Solar Cycle 3

This cycle marked the moment when humanity stopped treating the frontier as something merely to observe and began reorganizing civilization around reaching it. The ISA repeatedly favored decisions that accelerated outward expansion: self-healing LY-hulls moved rapidly into production, exploratory fleets multiplied, and the first dedicated wormhole expedition finally departed aboard the Silgean. Yet even as exploration surged ahead, old tensions refused to disappear. Bandwidth disputes, public distrust fueled by Bakht4Umayyad, and competition between scientific and industrial priorities followed the ISA into nearly every major decision, reminding everyone that the logistics of expansion remained as politically fraught as the discoveries themselves. The cycle also revealed a subtle shift in how evidence was valued. Earlier debates had often opposed firsthand experience to abstract theory; now the two increasingly reinforced one another. Amytis’s observations, Tekkalan’s analyses, laboratory materials science, and planetary engineering all became parts of the same conversation, each pushing the others forward. By the cycle’s close, the wormhole network had transformed from a provocative hypothesis into humanity’s gateway to the stars, and the ISA found itself confronting a new reality: the greatest challenge was no longer proving that the wider galaxy could be reached, but deciding how an increasingly divided civilization would choose to explore it.

Before the frontier could expand any farther, the ISA found itself dragged back into an old argument over scarce bandwidth. The latest quarrel—between an Umayyad cybernetics consortium and an Aksum agricultural hub—arrived with Bakht4Umayyad already inflaming public opinion. Malina Meriwa quietly disrupted communications that had enabled recent supply diversions, blunting one avenue of political maneuvering before the discussion truly began. K’Tinga opened by arguing that genetic cultivation deserved dependable access to the network, and Stacey soon added Gupta’s considerable weight behind that view. Unitus focused instead on explaining Achaemenia’s work to the wider public, while Silili Dashrapi, Kalo Meme Momo, and K’Tinga each began to encourage their own populations to adopt new genomic modifications reflecting their societies’ evolving priorities. As Inuit and Tu’i Tonga expanded their own capabilities in the background, no organized resistance ever gathered. The ISA reserved bandwidth for agricultural research, leaving frustrated cyberneticists to complain that their own movements now lagged behind the crops they helped sustain.

The discussion soon drifted back toward the Olwyn Cloud, where crews argued that longer voyages demanded changes to the explorers themselves. That proposal found remarkably little enthusiasm. Silili spoke first for improved spacecraft, Kalo quickly agreed, and Malina assembled an even broader case that exploration should advance through engineering rather than physiology. K’Tinga and Stacey added their voices with little hesitation, leaving no meaningful constituency for gene therapy. Unitus spent the session strengthening Achaemenia’s logistical capacity while Gupta quietly expanded its own industrial base and Scythia continued investing in extraction. Tu’i Tonga returned once more to the question of better hulls, making the consensus impossible to miss. The ISA ordered a new generation of lighter ships, and Liu Youwei’s self-healing LY-hulls soon turned that decision into the foundation of humanity’s next leap outward.

Fresh discoveries among the proto-comets prompted surprisingly little disagreement. Reports of unusual organics convinced nearly everyone that quick sampling mattered more than exhaustive cataloging. Kalo Meme Momo expressed that view first, Malina immediately echoed it, and K’Tinga pressed for rapid deployment while Stacey and Unitus each lent their support without embellishment. Silili Dashrapi arrived with the strongest endorsement of all, leaving the matter effectively settled before anyone could mount an alternative. While every polity quietly expanded its own production at home, the ISA dispatched biochemical microdrones into the densest regions of the Olwyn Cloud. They returned tantalizing hints of carbon chemistry, but nothing yet sufficient to explain how those compounds had come to be.

Another communications dispute proved far more theatrical. This time Mayan observatories and Incan construction fleets competed for the same transmission corridor, and no one doubted that the result would shape future exploration. Kalo spent the early stages patiently assembling support for uninterrupted scientific observations, persuading Silili Dashrapi to join that effort before the formal exchanges had fully unfolded. Malina answered with an equally energetic defense of the shipyards, and K’Tinga soon reinforced her position while drawing Stacey into the same camp through careful negotiation. Unitus became the obvious prize. Both sides courted Achaemenia with unusual intensity, and when Unitus finally declared for the observatories, every conversation in the room seemed to stop at once. Silili then exercised Scythia’s procedural authority to settle the remaining uncertainty in favor of the astronomers, ensuring that increasingly precise solar models would guide humanity’s future, even if the shipyards grumbled about every delayed weld.

With the first dedicated wormhole expedition approaching, attention shifted from politics to the crew that would cross the threshold. Tu’i Tonga attempted one last quiet conversation with Malina before the meeting began, but Inuit refused to budge. K’Tinga instead argued that only an AI could weigh the flood of information awaiting the mission, and Stacey committed Gupta firmly to that position. Unitus agreed without much ceremony, while Silili and Kalo concentrated on strengthening their own logistical preparations rather than reopening the strategic question. Malina likewise expanded Inuit’s capabilities instead of prolonging a debate that had largely run its course. The ISA filled the Silgean with unprecedented computational power, and humanity’s first systematic exploration of the wormhole network began under the watchful guidance of machines built to notice what people never could.

Even after the success of the LY-hulls, Tekkalan remained unwilling to let the material’s story end there. The AI celebrity urged the ISA to consider whether its remarkable self-repair might also transform living tissue. Silili Dashrapi preferred to put the alloy to work immediately, and Kalo, Malina, and eventually Stacey all arrived at the same conclusion after weighing the alternatives from different directions. Gupta took unusually long to commit, prompting no shortage of amused whispers around the table, but Stacey finally sided with rapid exploration. Unitus once again concentrated on expanding Achaemenia’s production capacity, while Oyo quietly prepared additional manufacturing of its own. By the close of the session, the ISA had committed the LY-hull almost entirely to starship construction. Within a year, exploration fleets were growing faster than anyone had imagined possible, even as engineers began noticing subtle distortions that hinted the remarkable material still held secrets of its own.
