Narrative—Game 1

A New Dawn Above Earth. At the beginning of a sunspot cycle, the Interpolity Science Assembly convenes in orbit for the first time. Its headquarters is a well-appointed suite of rooms in the Orbital Research Installation, a glimmering ring suspended high above the quiet curve of Earth. Envoys from six polities have been chosen to lead civilization through an age of discovery—and of growing peril. The Sun, once a symbol of renewal, has begun to falter in strange and unpredictable rhythms, casting flares that disrupt communication, damage infrastructure, and darken continents. Yet optimism runs high; humanity has never been so united or so capable. As this 1st Cycle begins, hope rises with it that understanding the rhythms of the star will illuminate the path ahead. Here aboard the ORI, science and diplomacy intertwine, and every decision the ISA makes shapes the survival of civilization.

The Tu’i Tonga crew that maintains the ORI’s power grid wants to expand the station’s overtaxed radioisotope thermoelectric generators. One downside is that the nearby investigations into enhanced-yield grains would be displaced. Do we protect the grain experiments, or allow the generator expansion and delay the genetic project?
—Power upgrades can be undertaken at any time. The genetic modification experiments, which are already starting to produce substantial grain yields from more compact planters, will be crucial to the expansion of human presence away from Earth. Their relocation would stall months of careful cultivation, so we keep the project rooted where it is.

The interpolity community is increasingly worried about extensive data corruption from heightened sunspot activity. Digitized archives show signs of degradation in several regions, while elders responsible for unrecorded oral histories warn that interruptions in communication networks are making it harder for communities to gather. Do we reinforce the digitized archives most at risk, or equip the oral historians to begin recording immediately?
—Every other system would be useless without the archived knowledge of humankind. We initiate a project to retrofit vulnerable data stores with magnetoresistant materials, directing fabrication hubs to prioritize replacement casings and dispatching inspection teams to stabilize the most fragile vaults.

Archives Reborn. Across the world’s archives, the great retrofit begins. Libraries, databanks, and memory vaults are stripped of vulnerable circuitry and reborn with magnetoresistant cores designed to preserve humanity’s knowledge for centuries. Omotanwa, an Oyo materials architect celebrated for transforming industrial compounds into instruments of endurance, oversees the global conversion from orbit. Her teams move with a precision that mirrors her own calm authority; each upgrade not only repairs a system but honors the generations that built it. As ancient servers flicker back to life beneath their new shielding, long-lost datasets and early records rejoin the living network. What began as a desperate safeguard becomes an act of renewal, binding the scattered history of humanity into a single, resilient memory.

As the newly upgraded archives near activation, temperature fluctuations in the lower vaults begin damaging restored materials. Do we implement an autonomous curation system developed by Seljuk data architects to safeguard the collections digitally, or expand power infrastructure proposed by Ascomanni hydromechanists to maintain environmental control?
—We can trust the data to guard itself. We authorize deployment of a self-indexing archivist trained on centuries of record fragments. Ait names aitself Safa and reorganizes holdings faster than any manual team, uncovering overlooked correlations across eras. Historiographers hail the precision, though classifications now shift frequently as the system refines aits logic.

Automated telescopes discover a faraway comet that could possibly be on a collision course with Earth. Experts, however, insist that there is nothing to fear—any orbit collision would have taken place aeons ago, long before humanity had reason to worry. Still, public curiosity spikes as preliminary trajectories circulate through newsfeeds, each model shaded with its own unsettling margin of error. Do we retask a satellite to monitor the comet, or trust the astronomers’ theory?
—The astronomers’ models already address the public concern. We allow satellites to continue their planned survey schedules, preserving the broader research cadence. To quiet lingering anxiety, we ask observatories to prepare a concise explanation of their orbital analysis and circulate it through public channels alongside a brief ISA statement affirming confidence in their work.

A Telescopic Expert. One of the experts in orbital mechanics, a Mayan named Xaman, successfully applies for a position on the ORI. They bring up from Earth an intrasystem telescope, linked to the world’s best astronomical databases, that can automatically track the movements of thousands of masses simultaneously. The instrument’s first calibration sweep lights up the ORI’s displays with shifting vectors from satellites, debris, and distant survey craft. Xaman will check in on the comet, which the Global Astronomical Society has named Fola Fuar, from time to time to confirm the experts’ optimism about its path; but most of their work will be concerned with other orbiting bodies.

Renewed fascination with Malmani’s origins is putting pressure on the ISA to determine once and for all whether the prophet was real or mythical. Do we fund a broad-spectrum genomic program that might reveal hidden patterns in ancient DNA, or sponsor a materials-based study analyzing medical relics and preserved remains to detect long-term shifts in human vitality?
—Pursuing the molecular path appeals to those who see life itself as history’s most robust ledger. We assemble teams to catalog fragmentary genomes from burial sites and museum specimens, developing algorithms to detect subtle inflection points across millennia. Each new sequence feels like a door half-open onto an era when humanity quietly changed.

True Beginnings. Year-long ISA projects across multiple polities converge on a single result. Genetic sampling from burial sites, medical relics, and preserved tissues shows that human lifespans lengthened sharply between twenty and ten centuries ago, rising across continents within just a few generations. Parallel isotope studies trace abrupt global surges in vitality, confirming this pattern. The data is too consistent for coincidence, too rapid for evolution, and too uniform for regional innovation. Within the ISA, quiet unease tempers the triumph—no mechanism explains how this change occurred. Still, the public is euphoric as newsfeeds proclaim the “Malmani era” real history, not parable. Spiritual communities hold vigils to honor proof that enlightenment once walked the Earth, and the ISA basks in global acclaim for settling the question that defined an age.

During the global retrofit, the Oyo materials specialist Omotanwa identifies subtle fluctuations in the magnetoresistant compounds that protect the new archival cores. Some see patterns worth explaining in the otherwise negligible variations. Do we instruct her to coordinate the modeling of the compounds’ long-term energy behavior, or authorize her to incorporate adaptive mechanical layers to compensate directly for any drift?
—The people on the ground need the tools to do their jobs. We authorize Omotanwa to embed responsive actuator networks linked to local control circuits, letting each casing provide limited sensory feedback and mechanical self-correction. The cybernetic shells maintain perfect equilibrium through field shifts, though technicians report the faint pulse of motion beneath their hands.

The astronomer Xaman reports a large number of outer solar system objects appearing in observation records. Their logs show more faint returns than the existing network can reliably classify, hinting at either sensor limitations or a genuine rise in distant activity. Do we commission them to develop a robust theory for their joint orbital mechanics, or initiate a program to provide low-albedo vision enhancement implants to current pilots?
—There are already plenty of potential spotters exploring commercial opportunities in the outer solar system. We contract for a supply of cybernetic optical grafts and subsidize them for trustworthy pilots who agree to sync their scans with our database. Clinics prepare their surgical queues as flight coordinators identify crews whose routes will immediately stress-test the upgraded vision.

Patches in the Cloud. Before long, firsthand surveillance of the distant edge of the system confirms that our solar system is surrounded by a vast torus of proto-cometary bodies, a hundred times more distant than Pluto and the Kauppias Belt of asteroids. This hypothetical region had been named the Olwyn Cloud by the Global Astronomical Society, but now it has been directly observed. As reports arrive from scattered patrols and long-range surveys, the scale of the structure becomes undeniable. After several months of aggregating other pilots’ data, one of the first to receive the optical implant, an Achaemenian named Amytis, notices that while the proto-comets display a wide variety of mineral compositions, the more unusual materials are consistently concentrated in certain patches of the Olwyn Cloud; this lack of uniformity cries out for an explanation.

As telescopes occasionally record streams of data on Fola Fuar’s metallic density and solar flux, research groups propose using the information for practical benefit. Do we adapt the findings to design a novel radiation-based energy source, or engineer microorganisms that could metabolize cometary metals for future mining?
—Power will open the frontier. We direct energy facilities to model reactors after the comet’s radiation patterns. Prototype collectors designed from the data show stunning efficiency but flicker unpredictably, following solar flares more closely than forecasts allow. Power surges ripple through orbital grids, thrilling engineers but briefly blacking out three communications satellites.
