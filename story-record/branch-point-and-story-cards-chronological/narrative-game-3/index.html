Narrative—Game 3

The Editable Genome. The newest generation of gene editors arrives with little notice: compact, reliable, and safe enough for routine use in neighborhood clinics. Suddenly, therapies that once belonged to affluent research centers become part of ordinary healthcare, and each society's long-held beliefs about change gain new weight. Central polity medical boards publish guidelines that differ sharply: some emphasize steady, minimal adjustments while others encour- age broader variation now that the tools are gentle and precise. Their citizenries begin adopting these recommendations, collectively reshaping their genetic profiles in ways that mirror cultural expectations.

An Umayyad cybernetics consortium reports bandwidth losses during peak transmission hours, claiming the neighboring Aksum agricultural hub is consuming signal space meant for industrial implants. Aksum insists the interference originates from Umayyad maintenance protocols; Bakht4Umayyad amplifies the dispute online, accusing the ISA of bias toward “organic” enterprises. Do we prioritize the consortium's continuous implant link, or guarantee the hub steady access to the frequencies needed for genetic cultivation and testing?
—Sustenance outranks convenience. We preserve bandwidth for the agricultural research servers, ensuring the genetic cultivars continue growing through precise remote adjustments. The Umayyad technicians lash back on public channels, insisting their own bodies lag mid-command—each motion now a reminder that progress moves at someone else's pace.

The Olwyn Cloud crews want to extend their voyages by reducing oxygen consumption, arguing that life support limits how long humans can stay in the field. Experienced shipbuilders counter that using next-generation hull composition will be far more efficient. Do we contract for gene therapy on spacers' lungs, or hold out for better materials and sleeker ships?
—The engineers' proposal seems more objective than the desires of the crew themselves. We direct the relevant alloy factories to prioritize lighter hull frames, improved seal tolerances, and redesigned internal tanks. New ships carry thicker walls and denser reserves, trading crew comfort for longer operational ranges without altering human physiology.

Self-Healing Hulls. After some time, we receive positive news from Cangmang Station, an advanced Han shipyard in Earth orbit. Its director Liu Youwei led the development of a new hull-plate substance made from germanium nanotubes threaded with ionomeric polymers; her material is able to repair itself in real time, making it ideal for resisting micropunctures. Test sections seal visible damage within seconds under simulated debris strikes, leaving only faint thermal traces behind. As a result, spaceships equipped with these “LY-hulls” are much lighter and thus capable of carrying more fuel for longer journeys into the outer system. Meanwhile, Tekkalan has been controlling aits impatience, as ait has pieced together strong circumstantial evidence of a large wormhole in the Olwyn Cloud in the direction of the galactic centre; the ISA is eager for firsthand confirmation.

As pilots' optical feeds highlight clusters of unusual elements within the Olwyn Cloud, experts Earthside open a debate about the cause of their uneven spread. Do we reinforce exploratory ships with sample drones immediately to test whether organic compounds concentrate alongside these anomalies, or design a more systematic survey using reinforced collection frames to chart every mineral variation in full detail?
—The fastest answers come from what's already reacting. We dispatch microdrones with biochemical sensors to sweep the densest pockets first. Their quick assays return faint traces of carbon chains and nitrates—intriguing but fragmentary, gone before full analysis. The results confirm that some organics are present but leave their formation process entirely unresolved.

Mayan resonance observatories modeling solar harmonics share an uplink with Incan construction fleets transmitting orbital fabrication data. Both blame the other for saturating the channel—simulations desync, shipyards stall, and rumors spread that the ISA favors research over labor. Do we reserve the corridor for the observatories' ongoing transmissions, or grant priority to the fleets whose project deadlines depend on constant feedback?
—The observatories are closer to predicting what's coming than anyone else. We grant them priority access, linking their arrays to adaptive relay AIs that steady the solar readings and cut through the noise. The models tighten, their graphs growing cleaner, but the Incan shipyards report short lags that throw timing off by seconds—enough to make every weld feel just a little out of sync.

The new spaceship configuration is paying dividends: a single large Han vessel with an LY-hull is being readied for a deep dive into the known wormhole, its launch window carefully calculated and its margin for error slim. To make the most important mission decisions, do we emphasize command crew with poly-heuristic grafts, or AI analysis modules?
—In unprecedented situations, human reactions are no match for the comprehensive intake of data that AIs provide. We outfit the ship with a staggering amount of computational power and install interconnected, state-of-the-art resolution systems on every deck, ensuring that no sensor reading, anomaly, or probabilistic branch goes unexamined.

Wormhole Explorers. Some two years or so after ISA approval, a massive Sigfrodr-drive vessel, the “Silgean”, passes through the most promising wormhole with its combination of human and AI crew. Fortunately messages can be sent faithfully back through the wormholes, and while the reports and discoveries are unpredictable, the information never fails to fascinate experts and laypeople alike. In addition to resource-rich bodies in orbit around stars, and planets and moons with varying suitability for human occupation, we learn that most star systems possess a cluster of wormholes on their own outer edges, through which even more stars can be reached. Despite occasional dead-ends and backtracking, the continuing exploration of the huge ship greatly increases humanity's motivation for expanding beyond our solar system.

With LY-hull production accelerating at the Han Cangmang Station, the AI celebrity Tekkalan presses the ISA to decide between rapid expansion or deeper study of the material's biological potential. Do we dedicate the new substance to shipbuilding for immediate exploration, or facilitate a scientific collaboration to test whether the hull's self-repair mechanisms could interact with living tissue for adaptive resilience?
—Our fleet can't confirm what it can't yet reach. We divert industrial resources toward scaling the LY-hull process, equipping a dozen new exploration fuselages within the year. The material performs flawlessly, though inspections reveal subtle warping along high-stress seams that will require periodic recalibration.
