Narrative—Game 4

The Sun’s Temper. As if the sunspot storms weren't enough: stellar physicists are now informing the ISA that massive solar flares will begin to be seen in the middle of sunspot cycles, and these flares are expected to worsen in the coming decades. Earth and ISA polity headquarters should be safe, but sensitive equipment further out will be in danger of being damaged or destroyed by the powerful bursts. Individual polities scramble to provide shielding materials to its off-Earth holdings, which is a supply-intensive endeavor. The ISA proposes a collaborative effort to maintain orbital neutrino diverters in various key locations; it remains to be seen whether all its members polities will partake in this shared responsibility.

The planetary scientist Fahuiki publishes a controversial theory about branched wormholes, which conjecturally could lead to multiple destinations. Do we instruct the Silgean to prioritize its travel to search for them, or work on novel ways to detect their proposed energy signatures in past and present scans?
—The Silgean is outfitted for nearly any eventuality, and that includes searching for direct confirmation of the branched wormhole theory, if such structures exist at all. We pass along Fahuiki's published parameters to the ship's systems, adjusting its navigation priorities to watch for anomalous splits as it proceeds, and wish it happy hunting

A Different Wormhole. As time passes, a pattern becomes familiar: The Silgean transits an Ellertin–Roinn bridge to yet another star system; it deploys survey drones to catalogue the other wormholes clustered nearby; the drones scan for the parameters predicted by the theory; and when no such parameters are found, the Silgean travels through one of the nearby wormholes to start the process over. Flight crews come to recognize the rhythm of deployment and recall, the slow accumulation of null results. Suddenly, apparent bad luck becomes a partial triumph, when a probe veers far off course only to detect a single wormhole on the other side of the planetless star Obisian. The energy signatures of this lone wormhole are unique in the database thus far, fluctuating in ways no model accounts for … but they also differ markedly from predicted parameters. Is it, or is it not, a branched wormhole?

The Aksum historian Hiwot questions whether the extraordinary synchrony among Malmani-era texts could reflect shared sensory experience rather than mere cultural exchange. Do we test this by commissioning responsive sensory frameworks that replicate ancient conditions of perception, or extend comparative genetic sampling to determine whether common neural traits shaped how early peoples received the teachings?
—If the words converged, perhaps perception did too. We instruct Achaemenian cyberartisans to build haptic resonance chambers that simulate ancient environmental stimuli—light, pressure, and vibration recorded in surviving pigments. Testers report shifts in recall and cadence, patterns close to the inscriptions' rhythm. The results show that reproducing an experience is simpler than explaining what it meant.

Omotanwa, the Oyo materials specialist, wants to study how so many regions progressed so rapidly after the first Malmani teachings spread. Do we let her initiate coordinated physical excavations to collect radiometric samples from early post-Malmani structures, or reconstruct that era's engineering methods through digital modeling of surviving design fragments and excavation data?
—The surest record of progress lies buried in what the ancients built. We fund synchronized digs across key sites, letting Omotanwa extract alloy cores, ceramics, and stone composites from ruins attributed to Malmani's successors. The first analyses suggest a surprising uniformity, the data aligning too neatly to dismiss as coincidence.

Shared Foundations. Across the post-Malmani world, coordinated studies by multiple polities reveal a striking pattern in early infrastructure. Radiometric dating of alloys, ceramics, and building foundations shows that technologies advanced within a narrow window of centuries—far faster than would be expected from their known resources. City plans align in repeating geometries, medical texts describe nearly identical compounds, and toolmaking standards converge across continents. The synchronization is unusually precise, the samples' dates clustering more tightly than statistical models predict. Analysts note the anomaly for later review, but the larger picture is clear: humanity's first cooperative age was real. The findings affirm the legend that Malmani's shared purpose overcame rivalry as the driver of civilization's progress.

As the Silgean transmits its unprecedented readings from the Obisian system, Han technicians monitoring its LY-hull sensors must choose which approach to pursue first. Do we prioritize an AI-led analysis to model the anomaly's parameters in real time, or pause to refine the governing theory of wormhole branching before applying the data?
—Understanding will hold longer if we build it piece by piece. We assign the analytic teams to re-derive the mathematics of branching from first principles, folding the Silgean's data into each revision. The eventual framework unites all known singularities under one set of equations, yet its last term still resists simplification—an empty variable that seems to wait for observation to define it.

With both Sigfrodr drives and Chiele replicators operating near their limits, microfissures are starting to form in key components whenever the two systems interact for resource transfer. Some propose immediate reinforcement of materials, while others see signs that biological factors might mitigate the stress. Do we strengthen the affected parts through new composite fabrication from Inuit, or investigate whether organic bonding agents could adaptively repair the damage over time?
—Materials will endure longer if they can learn to mend themselves. We commission bioengineers to cultivate protein-based binding compounds that weave into the existing alloys, guided by telemetry from Chiele replicators. Growth is slow, but the hybrid surfaces begin closing fissures autonomously, leaving faint organic tracer lines across the metal like veins under skin.

The Mayan physicist Xaman delivers a report extrapolated from astronomical records: they are rather certain that Fola Fuar won't hit Earth. However, it will pass very close—closer than the moon's orbit, close enough that any unusual activity would be easy to miss until the last moment. Do we hastily fabricate a probe to land on the comet, or simply continue with the telescope monitoring?
—Opportunities for close study of cometary masses don't get any better than this. We direct the ad hoc assembly of a lightweight probe and launch it toward Fola Fuar, hoping that its parameters allow it to successfully attach itself to the comet. Mission crews begin carving out the required lab time and scheduling the first trajectory simulations.

Landing on a Comet. A team is hastily assembled in the Incan city of Tylea to create a sturdy probe out of amorphous metal foam optimized for impact absorption. They launch it on an intercept course with Fola Fuar; as the world watches via live camera feed, the probe gently bounces on the comet, then deploys its grapples for a tenuous connection to its icy surface. A thin mist of disturbed dust drifts past the camera as the probe settles, giving researchers their first close look at the comet's texture. Preliminary analyses indicate that the core of the comet is unusually large and densely metallic, which provides more microgravity than expected to assist the probe. As Fola Fuar speeds toward the Sun, how it will transit through the outer planets on the rest of its journey remains unclear.

The comet lander's preliminary scans reveal a porous crust of metallic glass, dense with unfamiliar isotopes and volatile ice. Oyo extraction teams request authorization to begin experimental processing. Do we refine the material as a new fuel substrate, or study whether its embedded organics can seed adaptive biomineral composites?
—Fuel is a certain dividend of this project. We contract the Oyetunde Power Syndicate to convert the comet's glassy ore into a catalytic plasma feedstock. Early trials exceed yield projections but produce corrosive vapor that etches equipment and blinds sensors. The syndicate reports record output—followed by an unscheduled evacuation drill.

Energy diagnostics from synchronized excavation grids reveal faint interference between mineral deposits in early construction sites and the sensors used to date them, blurring isotope readings. The Aksum historian Hiwot argues the effect reflects something intrinsic to ancient materials themselves, while the Oyo materials specialist Omotanwa calls for technical correction. Do we pursue biological filtration to isolate the residues' elemental profile, or install a rapid computational filter to stabilize the signal?
—Accurate signal first, explanation later. We assign Gupta informaticians to implement a responsive analytic filter that detects mineral interference and counterbalances it in real time. Ait restores clarity almost instantly, allowing Omotanwa's teams to finalize their calibrations, though subsequent reviews note subtle variance near every waterlogged site.

Following rumors of a singularity in the Olwyn Cloud, the AI celebrity Tekkalan argues that the next step should be data integration rather than expansion, while field crews push for immediate exploration. Do we grant Tekkalan authority to unify the readings into a comprehensive theory of singularity formation, or allocate additional resources for extended propulsion and direct surveys?
—We'll lose this opportunity if we hesitate. We assign Incan powerwrights to propulsion array expansion and launch a new round of exploratory missions. Within the first expansion window, crews encounter three verified wormholes and transmit home telemetry that redefines the solar system's outer boundary.

Stations of Influence. Three new research hubs join the ORI: two cutting-edge orbital stations and a next-generation lunar base, each opening its docks to the ISA amid a surge of anticipation. Every polity gets ready to jockey for position, aware that command of the ORI brings rare prestige and plentiful resources, while leadership of a new orbital station offers a chance to champion a favored scientific direction from the outset. The lunar base, harder to supply but with abundant privacy, attracts those who value extended experimentation horizons or quiet strategic planning, far from the routine scrutiny of orbital traffic. In times of disruption like this, the logistical reshuffling only heightens the stakes: it is not uncommon for a prototype, a database, or a set of lab credentials to become "misplaced", reappearing months later in another polity's possession without a clear record of how it traveled. As preparations accelerate, interest sharpens and polite inquiries turn into carefully crafted overtures, each polity weighing which location might best anchor its ambitions for the decades to come.
