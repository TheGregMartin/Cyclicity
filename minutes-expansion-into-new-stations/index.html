Minutes—Expansion into new stations

The establishment of three new research stations marked far more than an administrative reorganization: it signaled humanity's emergence into a new era of solar-system civilization. After weeks of intense political maneuvering, Kalo Meme Momo secured leadership of the ORI, while Station α, Station β, and the Lunar Station each quickly developed distinct scientific identities under Igaluk Qillaq, G'Dath Pumwi, and Neil, respectively. Yet the formal appointments told only part of the story. Across the ISA, research teams, prototype technologies, archives, and administrative responsibilities quietly migrated between polities through a web of negotiations whose precise workings remained politely obscure. The most visible exception came when Silili Dashrapi unsuccessfully sought to rematriate the Scythian linguist Hajatria, exposing a rare public dispute over whether stewardship of a celebrated researcher belonged to the scholar's homeland or to the institution that currently supported his work. In retrospect, historians regard the entire transition as the moment humanity ceased to organize itself around a single planet and instead began treating the solar system itself as the natural home of its laboratories, observatories, and ambitions.

The opening of three new research stations transformed what might have been a routine administrative reshuffle into one of the ISA's most closely watched political contests. Delegations drifted between receptions, technical briefings, and late-night conversations, each convinced that the institutions founded now would shape scientific priorities for decades to come. By the time the discussions settled, each station had begun to acquire a distinct identity. Kalo Meme Momo narrowly secured stewardship of the ORI despite determined competition from Silili Dashrapi, assuming the additional responsibilities of station chief while continuing to represent Tu’i Tonga within the ISA. Station α quickly acquired a cybernetic character under the stewardship of the Inuit researcher Igaluk Qillaq, appointed by Malina Meriwa to guide the new facility from its first day of operation. K‘Tinga likewise established materials science as the defining pursuit of Station β, entrusting it to the Oyo lepidopterist-turned-administrator G'Dath Pumwi. The fiercest contest unfolded over the secluded Lunar Station, where Unitus von Standard-Issue ultimately prevailed after a crowded struggle and a near miss of Stacey’s attempt to control the station. The Achaemenian Neil became chief of the Lunar Station; what most observers regarded as a prestigious command, Achaemenians quietly understood as a punishment, consigning him to a station that was difficult to supply but uniquely suited to long-term experiments, discreet observation, and work beyond the constant attention of the ORI.

Leadership appointments alone, however, tell only part of the story. As laboratories changed hands and administrative responsibilities were rewritten, specialist teams, prototype technologies, privileged databases, and long-negotiated research agreements developed an uncanny tendency to appear under new stewardship. Official memoranda invariably justified every transfer, though few observers could later reconstruct exactly how each one had come about—or why no one involved ever seemed especially eager to do so. Inuit emerged as the quiet beneficiary of this new political climate, gradually becoming steward to an extraordinary collection of the ISA's most distinctive individuals and programs, including responsibility for monitoring Bakht4Umayyad. Oyo quietly accumulated practical capabilities that strengthened its industrial future, while Scythia quietly secured responsibility for one of the ISA's most promising cybernetics programs after a closely contested exchange. Tu’i Tonga accepted several such losses with surprising composure, sacrificing specialized institutional advantages while ensuring that the ORI itself—and the authority to guide humanity's scientific agenda—remained in Kalo's hands.

One dispute nevertheless escaped the usual bureaucratic haze. Silili Dashrapi made a determined effort to rematriate the Scythian linguist Hajatria, arguing that one of Scythia's foremost scholars ought to continue his work under Scythian stewardship. Unitus resisted just as firmly, insisting that Hajatria's existing arrangements should remain undisturbed. Neither questioned the importance of his research; the disagreement lay in who should oversee it, and who would ultimately earn the distinction of supporting one of the ISA's most celebrated investigators. Achaemenia's position prevailed, leaving Hajatria where he was. Among Scythian circles, however, quiet concern lingered for Hajatria's wellbeing, and for whether political posturing had begun to outweigh the wishes of the scholar himself.

Looking back, historians often identify this reorganization as the moment humanity quietly abandoned the assumptions of a terran age. Earth remained the cradle of civilization, but no longer its unquestioned center of gravity. Scientific leadership had become distributed across orbital stations, the Moon, distant expeditions, and the growing web of institutions that connected them. The solar system itself had become humanity's natural arena for discovery, administration, and ambition. The new stations did more than expand the ISA; they reflected a civilization that had begun to think of itself not as a world occasionally reaching outward, but as a people whose laboratories, observatories, and ambitions now belonged to the solar system as naturally as they once had to Earth alone.
