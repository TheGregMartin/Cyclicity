The solar system

The definitions of the solar system, and the bodies within it, are shaped by a sometimes clashing combination of mythology, historical convention, and modern scientific consensus.

The modern definition of a planet is a body in the solar system orbiting the Sun that is larger than every satellite of any other solar system body. In practice, this means that the planets are the bodies larger than Ganymede, the largest of Jupiter’s satellites. Consequently, there are seven recognized planets: Venus, Earth, and Mars in the inner solar system, and Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune in the outer solar system.

Other prominent bodies include the solar satellites, defined to be bodies too small to be a planet that nevertheless orbit the Sun and have gravitationally rounded themselves. Mercury and Ceres are the unique solar satellites in the inner solar system and the asteroid belt, respectively. There are nine known solar satellites beyond the outer solar system: Eris, Gonggong, Haumea, Makemake, Orcus, Pluto, Quaoar, Salacia, and Sedna.

The asteroid belt between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter marks the border between the inner and outer solar systems. Beyond the outer solar system is the Kauppias belt, a vast ring of asteroids and protocomets, some of which occasionally have their orbits disturbed and head closer to the sun as a comet. Farther still is the heliosphere, the region where charged particles from the sun’s corona become sparse enough to be overcome by interstellar matter. Surrounding all of this is believed to be the Olwyn Cloud, a sphere of rocky and icy bodies extending half a light-year out; astronomy is just now reaching the point where direct observation of the Olwyn Cloud should become possible.
