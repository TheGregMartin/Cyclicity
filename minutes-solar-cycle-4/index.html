Minutes—Solar Cycle 4

This solar cycle was defined by a growing confidence that humanity had crossed a threshold. The great questions before the ISA no longer centered on whether the frontier beyond the solar system could be reached, but on how quickly its discoveries could be understood before the frontier expanded again. The Silgean's steady progress transformed speculation into routine: survey followed survey, theories matured alongside observations, and each answer seemed only to expose another layer of mystery. Even Fola Fuar, once merely a distant curiosity causing a fleeting public anxiety, became a destination in its own right as humanity successfully landed on its surface and began extracting both scientific insight and practical value from the encounter. Yet the cycle also carried an unmistakable current of cooperation—not the seemingly effortless unanimity celebrated in histories of the Malmani era, but a more pragmatic willingness to negotiate, exchange favors, and occasionally postpone today's disagreement in return for tomorrow's support. Political obligations became an accepted part of the ISA's culture, quietly shaping decisions alongside public argument. At the same time, concern for humanity's long-term resilience widened beyond exploration alone. Preparations for worsening solar flares, continuing refinement of each polity's own population through voluntary genomic change, and renewed excavations into humanity's first great age all reflected a civilization becoming increasingly conscious that its future would be measured not only by how far it traveled, but by how well it prepared itself for the journey.

Fahuiki's latest theory found the ISA in an unusually unified mood. Rather than waiting for new instruments to sift through old observations, Silili Dashrapi argued that the Silgean itself should become the test, searching actively for the branched wormholes her fellow Tu’i Tongan had predicted. K’Tinga quickly agreed, Unitus lent Achaemenia's support without hesitation, and Stacey saw little reason to slow the expedition. Malina Meriwa and Kalo Meme Momo spent the session cultivating future political understandings instead of pressing competing proposals, while Gupta quietly disrupted one of the logistical incentives that had recently encouraged especially ambitious ISA projects. With no serious opposition, the Silgean departed under revised instructions to watch for branching paths wherever its journey might lead. The result was not the confirmation Fahuiki had anticipated, but something stranger still: a solitary wormhole circling the lonely star Obisian, unlike anything yet recorded.

The search for Malmani's world turned inward once more when Hiwot wondered whether humanity's ancestors had shared not merely ideas but perception itself. Malina Meriwa immediately embraced the possibility, arguing that recreating ancient experience might reveal what centuries of scholarship had missed. Kalo joined her with equal conviction, while Oyo and Gupta countered that inherited neurological traits deserved closer examination instead. Between the two camps, Unitus quietly redirected resources away from Tu’i Tonga towards Achaemenia, before assuming the newly vacant office of Patent Agent, leaving everyone keenly aware that the final decision might rest with them. Silili Dashrapi meanwhile paused long enough to strengthen the ISA's common solar-flare defenses before returning to Scythia's own affairs. As the debate deadlocked, Malina privately appealed to the Patent Agent with unusual determination, outweighing K’Tinga's own last-minute overtures. Unitus's ruling finally sent the ISA toward immersive haptic reconstructions, leaving researchers able to reproduce ancient sensations even if their deeper meaning remained elusive.

Omotanwa's proposal produced perhaps the closest thing to unanimity the ISA had enjoyed in some time. Stacey arrived armed with an exhaustive case for coordinated excavations, and Gupta's enthusiasm quickly became infectious. Unitus amplified that momentum with characteristic certainty, while Malina, Kalo, and Silili all found little reason to resist. K’Tinga quietly continued Oyo's long-term reshaping of its own population even as the polity championed the excavations it had inspired, and Scythia pursued similar domestic reforms between exchanges. A few small maneuvers continued in the background—Gupta quietly appropriated Achaemenian logistical assets, Tu’i Tonga contributed to the growing solar-flare initiative, and Inuit expanded its own institutions—but the central question had already been settled. The synchronized excavations confirmed what many had hoped but scarcely expected to prove: humanity's first great flourishing had advanced with astonishing unity across continents.

Fresh reports from the Silgean soon forced the ISA back to Obisian. The new measurements tempted some toward immediate computational analysis, yet this time the room displayed remarkable patience. Stacey opened by arguing that sound theory should precede clever algorithms, and Kalo, Silili, and Malina each reinforced that position from different directions. Before long, the discussion had shifted from whether theory was worthwhile to how thoroughly it should be pursued. K’Tinga concentrated on strengthening Oyo's domestic institutions while Unitus quietly guided Achaemenia's own genetic development and reclaimed valuable resources from Gupta. As the conversation wound down, Gupta joined the expanding effort to shield orbital infrastructure against future solar flares. The ISA ultimately chose to rebuild the mathematics from first principles, producing elegant equations that explained almost everything—save for one stubborn variable that seemed to await a discovery not yet made.

The next engineering problem divided the ISA in an unexpected way. Protein-based repair compounds attracted immediate support from Tu’i Tonga, Scythia, Oyo, and eventually Gupta, all persuaded that living systems might succeed where conventional metallurgy had begun to fail. Malina Meriwa surprised nearly everyone by making the strongest case of the day for improved composite materials instead. More than one observer later remarked that they had expected Inuit to favor the biological approach, and even Silili reportedly admitted her astonishment when Malina's position became clear. Unitus spent the session frustrating one of Oyo's domestic initiatives rather than joining the central dispute, while Tu’i Tonga and Scythia quietly reinforced their own long-term capabilities. In the end, the broader coalition prevailed, and the ISA commissioned organic bonding agents whose slow, almost lifelike repairs left metallic surfaces threaded with delicate veins.

The return of Fola Fuar to the ISA agenda revived memories of humanity's earliest debates, but the mood had changed completely. Fear had given way to opportunity. Malina Meriwa argued passionately that no telescope could rival a probe resting on the comet itself, and K’Tinga quickly joined her. Unitus remained the lone public advocate for continued observation, preferring disciplined patience over hurried improvisation, while Gupta, Tu’i Tonga, and Scythia all devoted the meeting largely to strengthening their own institutions and supply networks rather than contesting the expedition. The outcome never seriously came into doubt. Engineers hastily assembled a lightweight lander, and before long the world watched it bounce gently onto Fola Fuar's icy surface, opening an entirely new chapter in cometary exploration.

The first results from the lander immediately sparked fresh ambitions. Stacey argued that the comet's metallic glass promised practical dividends if refined into an energy source, and Gupta returned repeatedly to that theme until few doubted the opportunity. Silili Dashrapi reinforced the case with unusual vigor, while Kalo offered Tu’i Tonga's support without much ceremony. Malina and Tu’i Tonga each continued guiding the long-term evolution of their own populations during pauses in the discussion, and Oyo concentrated on expanding the industrial base that would eventually exploit whatever decision emerged. Unitus quietly broadened Achaemenia's logistical reach instead. By the close of the session, catalytic plasma feedstock had won the day. The new process yielded astonishing amounts of energy, although the evacuation drills that soon followed reminded everyone that remarkable discoveries rarely arrived without fresh complications.

The ISA's final archaeological debate of the cycle unfolded beneath the growing shadow of solar storms. Gupta and Tu’i Tonga quickly aligned behind Omotanwa's call for computational filtering, arguing that clean measurements had to come before deeper interpretation. Malina accepted that reasoning with little fuss, while Oyo focused on strengthening its own institutions. Silili delayed entering the discussion while Scythia's private intelligence network quietly gathered one last round of information. The pause provoked an extended conversation that soon wandered away from archaeology altogether and toward the broader question of whether every polity truly owed the same degree of cooperation against the worsening flares. Unitus even hinted that Hajatria's future assignments might depend upon Scythia's choice, and more than one private appeal crossed the room before the matter ended. In the end, Silili chose to reinforce the common mitigation effort despite the competing pressures surrounding her. The analytic filters restored clarity to the archaeological record, and Omotanwa closed a distinguished career knowing her final project had left the evidence sharper than she had found it.

The cycle concluded where it had so often begun: with Tekkalan urging restraint while explorers asked for one more journey into the unknown. Kalo spoke first for expanding the surveys, and Malina followed with unmistakable enthusiasm, insisting that opportunities like these would not wait for perfect theory. K’Tinga soon aligned with them, while Unitus alone argued that the accumulating observations deserved unification before humanity rushed farther outward. Stacey declined to join the central dispute, tending instead to Gupta's growing research networks as Scythia and Tu’i Tonga quietly expanded their own logistical reach. The ISA chose motion over hesitation. New propulsion systems carried fresh expeditions into the Olwyn Cloud, and within the first exploration window they discovered three additional wormholes, once again proving that every answer merely widened the frontier beyond it.
